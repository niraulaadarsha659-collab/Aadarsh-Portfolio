We Don’t Need More Rules for Fair Exams — We Need Students Watching the System

Published on Medium • By Aadarsh Niraula

Every exam season in Nepal, the same quiet routine repeats itself: answer sheets circulate before the bell rings, invigilators look the other way, and students who study honestly watch others cheat their way to the same grade. Most people who have gone through the system have a story like this — I have several. What surprised me wasn't that it happens. It's how little anyone expects it to change.

I started paying closer attention to this after realizing how much infrastructure already exists to prevent it — monitoring cameras installed and unused, funds allocated for exam integrity and left untouched, student bodies like Bal Clubs registered on paper but inactive in practice. The tools aren't missing. The oversight is.

What a small pilot showed

In Belbari, a group of us worked with local student clubs and coordinated directly with the Commission for the Investigation of Abuse of Authority (CIAA) to activate exactly the oversight that was supposed to already be in place: reactivating dormant Bal Clubs, getting students themselves involved in monitoring exam conduct and school ethics, and opening a direct line to authorities when something needed reporting. In the exam cycles after we started, malpractice reports in the area dropped noticeably — informal tracking put the decline at roughly 60%.

I want to be careful about what I'm claiming here. This was a small, local pilot, not a controlled study, and I don't have independent audit data to back the exact figure. What I can say with confidence is that when students had a real channel to flag problems and authorities were actually listening, behavior changed fast. That's the part worth taking seriously, regardless of the precise number.

Why this matters beyond one town

The cost of exam malpractice isn't just an unfair grade. It's:

  • Demotivation — honest students who study hard watch the system reward shortcuts instead, and many stop trying to compete fairly.
  • A widening skill gap — students who are pushed through without being properly evaluated move into higher education or the workforce without the competencies their certificates claim they have.
  • Eroding trust — parents and communities that no longer believe school outcomes reflect real learning have no reason to invest in the system, financially or otherwise.
  • A slow-motion brain drain — capable students who see the system as rigged increasingly look abroad for opportunities they feel they can't get fairly at home.

None of this is unique to one district. It's a structural problem, and structural problems need structural fixes — not just stricter rules on paper, which is largely what already exists and isn't being enforced.

What actually seems to work

Based on what we saw in Belbari, a few principles stand out:

  1. Put oversight in the hands of the people closest to the problem. Students notice more than adults assume, and they have the most at stake in a fair outcome. Active, properly registered Bal Clubs — not clubs that exist only in a registry — are a low-cost way to build in real-time accountability.
  2. Use the tools that already exist. Monitoring devices, allocated integrity funds, and reporting channels don't need to be invented — they need to be turned on and actually used.
  3. Make the link to authorities direct and fast. A report that takes weeks to reach anyone who can act on it isn't oversight, it's paperwork. Coordination with bodies like the CIAA and local education offices needs to be a standing relationship, not a one-time escalation.
  4. Track where funds and resources actually go. Transparency dashboards and community verification aren't bureaucratic overhead — they're the difference between money that improves learning and money that quietly disappears.

What I'd ask for

I don't think this needs a new government program or a large budget. It needs someone — a ministry office, a district education committee, an NGO already working in this space — willing to treat youth-led oversight as infrastructure, not an afterthought. Register the clubs. Fund the awareness campaigns. Keep the reporting line to authorities open and responsive. Belbari suggests it doesn't take much to move the needle. It takes willingness.

Fair exams shouldn't be the exception that requires a pilot project to achieve. They should be the baseline. The question is whether we're willing to actually use what's already sitting on the shelf.
